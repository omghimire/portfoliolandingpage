Protected Areas and Wildlife Corridors

Published on July 29, 2026

Protected areas are one of the oldest and most effective tools used to conserve biodiversity. National parks, wildlife reserves, conservation areas, and community-managed forests provide safe spaces where ecosystems can function with less interference from hunting, logging, or large-scale development. Yet biodiversity conservation today is not limited to drawing boundaries around a place. Species survival often depends on whether they can move safely between these protected zones.

Wildlife corridors are strips of habitat that link isolated natural areas, allowing animals to migrate, find mates, access food, and escape from disturbance. These corridors may be forests along rivers, mountain passes, grasslands, or community-managed land that remains connected across a landscape. They are especially important for large mammals, birds, and amphibians that need more space than a single reserve can provide. Without such links, populations become small and genetically isolated, making them more vulnerable to disease and climate stress.

In practice, building strong protected-area systems means combining enforcement with local cooperation. Communities living near corridors may need support for agriculture, water access, and livelihoods so that they can coexist with wildlife rather than compete with it. When protected zones and corridors are planned together, they create a broader ecological network that sustains biodiversity over the long term.
