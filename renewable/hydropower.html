Hydropower and run-of-river systems

Published on July 28, 2026

Hydropower has played a foundational role in renewable electricity for over a century because it turns the natural movement of water into dependable power. Large dams can generate stable electricity for cities and industries, while run-of-river systems offer a smaller-scale option that uses the flow of rivers with less storage and fewer large reservoirs. This makes hydropower especially valuable in places that need reliable power around the clock, because unlike solar or wind it can often respond quickly to demand. For many regions, especially those with high rainfall, mountainous terrain, or strong river systems, hydropower remains one of the most established renewable technologies available. Yet its benefits come with serious trade-offs. Large dams can alter river ecosystems, affect fish migration, reduce sediment flow, and displace communities when reservoirs expand. Because of this, modern hydropower planning increasingly depends on environmental assessments, community consultation, and new design strategies that protect aquatic life and maintain ecological balance. Run-of-river systems are often promoted as a gentler alternative because they do not require the same scale of storage, but they still need careful siting and water management to avoid negative effects. Climate change may also influence long-term performance because shifting rainfall and snowmelt patterns can change water availability. This means hydropower must be managed with flexibility and regional planning rather than treated as a fixed source of energy. When designed responsibly, hydropower can remain an important part of a balanced clean energy mix.
