Energy-efficient buildings and retrofits

Published on July 28, 2026

Energy-efficient buildings are a crucial part of climate action because the built environment consumes a large share of the world’s electricity and heat. Reducing energy demand in homes, offices, schools, and hospitals can directly lower emissions while also reducing utility costs and improving comfort. Efficiency upgrades often begin with insulation, air sealing, efficient glazing, and better ventilation systems, but they can also include high-performance lighting, smart thermostats, and modern appliances. These measures work together to reduce heating and cooling needs, and they make it easier for buildings to integrate renewable sources such as rooftop solar or heat pumps. Retrofitting older buildings is especially important because many structures were designed before energy performance standards became common, and their inefficiency can be very high. In many cities, retrofits are also tied to public health because better insulation and airflow can improve indoor air quality and reduce the risks of dampness and overheating. Yet one of the biggest obstacles to retrofit programs is cost. A building owner may need significant upfront funding even though long-term savings are strong. This is why governments, lenders, and utilities often create financing mechanisms, incentives, and building standards to accelerate upgrades. When energy efficiency is treated as a first step rather than an afterthought, it becomes one of the most practical and affordable routes to sustainability.
