Green hydrogen and energy storage

Published on July 28, 2026

Green hydrogen is attracting increasing attention because it offers a way to turn renewable electricity into a storable fuel that can be used later in sectors that are difficult to electrify directly. The process begins with electrolysis, where water is split into hydrogen and oxygen using electricity. If that electricity comes from solar, wind, or hydro, the resulting hydrogen can be considered low-carbon. This gives hydrogen special value in heavy industry, long-distance transport, shipping, aviation, and seasonal energy storage, where direct electricity use may not be practical. It can also help absorb surplus renewable power during periods of high generation and low demand, turning intermittent energy into a flexible resource. This makes green hydrogen especially relevant in countries that want to build large-scale renewable systems without relying entirely on batteries. However, the technology still faces major economic and technical barriers. Electrolyzers remain expensive, hydrogen storage needs specialized infrastructure, and transportation introduces efficiency losses. For these reasons, green hydrogen is not a universal replacement for electricity; it is better understood as a strategic option for particular applications. Governments and private companies are investing heavily in pilot plants, port facilities, and industrial projects to lower costs and improve performance. If these efforts scale successfully, hydrogen could become a bridge between renewable electricity and sectors that have been difficult to decarbonize. Its future depends not only on engineering innovation but also on smart policy, international cooperation, and strong investment planning.
